OVERVIEW

Factory training needed clearer guidance and consistent UI

SK On is a global EV battery manufacturer that trains production workers through a virtual simulator before they enter the factory floor. The project focused on redesigning its HMI training interfaces for workers with limited factory equipment experience.

However, text-heavy instructions and inconsistent layouts made training difficult, while hundreds of screens across machines and languages were hard to manage. As a UI/UX designer on a three-person design team, I redesigned training interactions into guided, game-like experiences and developed reusable UI components.

The redesign doubled learner satisfaction, cut training time by 8 hours, and reduced negative feedback by 33%.

CONTEXT ABOUT SK ON

A fast-growing battery maker needed workers ready for the line

SK on, the EV battery maker and a sister company of SK hynix, is expanding its plants across North America, Europe, and Asia. Every new plant needs trained production workers before they can start on the production line.

SERVICE INTRODUCTION

Every worker must pass a virtual training simulator first

Before working on site, production workers train on SK On's Virtual Training Simulator, an internal e-learning platform. Its simulation-based lessons and quizzes cover HMI operation, workplace rules, and key production procedures.

PROJECT BACKGROUND

78% had never used factory equipment, and 62.8% found the training hard to apply

The most common education level among learners was elementary school, and many had worked in farming before seeing factory equipment for the first time. The existing platform did not account for these backgrounds, so the training was hard to understand and apply on the job. A redesign needed to make the content accessible and engaging for these learners.

THE CHALLENGE
LEARNER CHALLENGE 01

Dense text and shifting layouts made training hard to follow

The training relied heavily on text, and each module copied a different machine's screen layout. Learners had to read long instructions while relearning the screen in every module, so key information was hard to find.

LEARNER CHALLENGE 02

Similar-looking items and unclear action cues caused a 72% misclick rate

Neither the character nor the instructions showed what to select next, so learners often picked the wrong item and got stuck.

PM CHALLENGE

Text-heavy screens were difficult to manage across languages

Explaining complex equipment took a lot of text. Translated text varied in length by language, and with so many screens, the workload grew beyond the team's limited time and resources.

Learners needed less text, while the team needed less complexity

DESIGN GOAL

Guide learners clearly while making training easier to scale

Show learners what to do at each step without dense instructions, and let the team expand training across processes and languages.

DESIGN STRATEGY

Character-led gameplay for learners, one reusable UI for the team

Rather than copying each machine interface, we built every learning step around three questions: What am I learning? What should I do next? What information matters now?

01

Turn instructions into character-led, guided gameplay

Use character-led game and visual feedback to make each action easy to follow instead of relying on text-heavy screens.

02

Standardize the UI across processes

Create one consistent structure and reusable components that could adapt across different processes.

TRADE-OFF

We prioritized learning effectiveness and scalability over copying each machine interface

01

Learners needed the workflow, not the exact screen

Since button names and the order of steps mattered most, a consistent UI let learners repeat them throughout.

02

Learners needed only a few buttons, not the full panel

A consistent layout and numbered steps helped build confidence and immersion.

03

A shared framework fit our tools and timeline

We built in Unity, where Figma files were hard to reuse, so one shared UI served different machines on a tight timeline.

FINAL DESIGN 1

Making equipment selection clear in a character-led experience

Although the game centered on a character, item selection needed to stay the visual priority. Internal feedback showed the 3D character pulled attention from the item cards, so I flattened the character and gave the cards more depth and contrast.

Replacing text-heavy instructions with a dress-up game

A dress-up game guided learners through the correct order for putting on safety gear. Pairing each instruction with a matching image helped them understand, remember, and follow the steps.

FINAL DESIGN 2

Balance game-like visuals with clear controls

Without adding new functions, we restyled existing components so key controls were easier to spot.
The goal was to help learners focus on and remember the controls they'd use on site.

OPTION  A

Playful, but visually busy

The game-like feel remained, but bright colors and metallic effects competed for attention.

OPTION B

Restrained, but too heavy

Limited color emphasized key controls, but the dark style felt heavy and monotonous.

SELECTED DIRECTION

Light, with selective emphasis

A light base retained the game-like feel. Size, depth, and limited color emphasized key controls.

Using a consistent layout to highlight what matters at each step

Each screen highlighted only what learners needed for that step and faded the rest. This consistency reduced confusion for learners and made screens easier for the team to build across processes. Keeping text minimal also made localization easier.

BEFORE

Copying machine screens exactly confused learners

Inconsistent colors and visual hierarchy made screens across processes feel like separate services.

AFTER

Color and numbered labels highlighted key areas

Numbered labels showed learners the order of actions.

Building a reusable component set to scale across machines

Shared layouts kept the training consistent. Defined component sizes
and states let the team adapt screens without rebuilding the UI each time.

LESSONS LEARNED 01

Reconstruct screens around learning goals, not the real machine

Machine interfaces varied across the workplace, and copying them exactly did not make learning easier. So we rebuilt each screen around what learners needed at that step. I learned that good training keeps the real workflow but removes the real complexity.

02

Build a scalable system to respond to change

We built hundreds of screens across processes, and requirements kept changing. Shared layouts and reusable components let us respond quickly without starting over. I learned that building an adaptable structure comes before perfecting each screen.
