OVERVIEW

Moving key information upfront and cutting site weight by 17×

The result: 17× lighter, 2.6× lower bounce rate, and 21% more engagement with key information.
MemeCore is a Web3 startup building a sustainable meme coin ecosystem around M. Its visually bold website helped the company stand out, but as MemeCore grew, investors and partners struggled to understand its value and credibility. Heavy assets also caused long load times, preventing some users from entering the site.

As the sole product designer and product manager, I redesigned the website to balance brand identity, information clarity, and performance. I also took the initiative to introduce vibe coding, which later evolved into a company-wide designer–developer workflow.

ABOUT  MEMECORE

A Web3 company building
an ecosystem around M coin

MemeCore is a Web3 startup behind M, a community-driven meme coin built to address trust issues in the meme coin market. Beyond the coin, MemeCore builds platforms and experiences that connect its community and drive long-term engagement.

THE IMPORTANCE OF SITE

Company growth shifted the website from visual impact to clear information

Early on, MemeCore used flashy GIF illustrations to stand out in the crowded Web3 market, helping grow its community to 600K+ and service participation to 3.2M+ before the M token launched. As MemeCore grew through major exchange listings, the website needed to shift from capturing attention to communicating value and building credibility.

PROBLEM

Investors and partners struggled to understand MemeCore and its direction

Despite MemeCore’s growth, skepticism around meme coins remained. Investors and partners repeatedly told the Growth & Marketing team that MemeCore and its direction were unclear. Reviewing the site revealed limited key information and no clear hierarchy, forcing the team to repeatedly explain the project in meetings.

DATA ANALYSIS & CHALLENGES

Unclear content structure and heavy page weight
drove a 56.1% initial bounce rate

Six months of Google Analytics and Microsoft Clarity data showed that users either left the first page without taking action or dropped off before it finished loading. As the PM and product designer, I tackled both design and development challenges, improving user experience and web performance together.

PROBLEM 1

Hidden navigation and no clear starting point

The first screen showed only graphics, with no navigation or key information. Pages could only be found through an unfamiliar hover interaction, so users dropped off before reaching key content.

PROBLEM 2

Oversized images and videos slowing the site

At 48.6MB, the site was 16× heavier than an average desktop page and needed an extra loading screen. On weaker networks, this made the site hard to even open, so optimization was essential.

INSIGHT

Users stayed 5× longer once they reached detail pages

The content itself was worth reading, but many users left before ever reaching it. As the business strategy became clearer after launch, I saw an opportunity to surface key information earlier and make it easier to navigate.

GOAL

Help investors reach key information more easily, increasing detail-page visits by 15% and reducing loading drop-off by 10%.

HYPOTHESIS
FOR PROBLEM 1

If we add a clear landing page and business-focused navigation, users will reach key information more easily.

FOR PROBLEM 2

If we replace heavy GIFs with CSS animations and WebP images, the site will load faster and reduce drop-off.

PRIORITIZATION

With many possible changes, I prioritized what mattered most

I mapped the changes that could test our hypotheses, then prioritized them by impact and effort to focus our limited resources on the highest-impact work.

DESIGN IMPROVEMENT 1

Building global navigation around user needs and evolving business priorities

The original site had no navigation bar, so key information was scattered and hard to reach. To build a clear hierarchy, I analyzed users' most frequent questions, MemeCore's business model, and Web3 services like Sui, Monad, and Pudgy Penguins to find common priorities and navigation patterns. I introduced the site's first navigation bar and refined it through two more releases as user needs and business priorities changed.

DESIGN IMPROVEMENT 2

Turning a visual-first homepage into an information-first landing page

Users could only find pages by hovering over illustrations, so key information stayed hidden. To decide what the homepage should show first, I worked with the Growth and Marketing teams to rank users' most common questions and the company's key messages, then built the new landing page around that order.

BEFORE

Pages were hidden inside an illustrated map and appeared only on hover, making key content hard to find.

AFTER

Key projects and business messages appear in order of importance, each linked to its detail page.

TRADE-OFF

Keeping the brand distinctive while making information clearer

Simplifying the visuals made information clearer, but the illustration team worried it would weaken MemeCore's identity. So instead of removing illustrations, I aligned the team on giving each one a clear role based on the section's goal: interaction where we needed attention, and simpler illustrations where we needed understanding.

1. Meaningful scroll interactions to guide attention

Many users left the first page without taking action, so I used scroll interactions only at moments where we needed to hold their attention.

Opening: Scroll motion draws users into the story so they keep scrolling past the first screen.

$M exchange: A second interaction brings attention back to the $M exchange section, a business-critical part of the site.

2. Consistent layouts where understanding matters

In information-heavy sections, I removed decorative interactions and used one layout: a key message, a short description, a clear CTA, and an illustration that shows what the content means. With less visual noise, users could scan faster while the brand stayed recognizable.

DESIGN RESULT

I compared three months before and after the redesign, with similar traffic in both periods. After the landing page became information-first, fewer users left immediately, and more continued to detail pages.

SHIPPING PROCESS
NEW CHALLENGE

After launch, the site still felt slow: 17% of visitors rage-clicked, and performance scored 55

We had already shrunk the GIF assets, but performance issues remained after launch. Microsoft Clarity showed about 17% of visitors repeatedly clicking with no response, and session replays showed users on slower networks struggling to load the page. A Lighthouse performance score of 55 confirmed we needed to go further.

EXECUTION STRATEGY

When our frontend developer was reassigned, I built the site myself with vibe coding

Midway through the project, our frontend developer moved to another urgent initiative. Instead of pausing or cutting scope, I took over implementation: I connected Claude Code to the Figma API and GitLab, built the frontend, and later handled some backend work. Testing designs directly in the browser cut handoffs and rework.

IMPLEMENTATION DECISION

Replacing full-scene GIFs with static WebP and CSS motion to cut page weight

Because I owned the code, I could fix this directly instead of waiting for a handoff. Each scene was a single heavy GIF that couldn't be optimized further, so I split it into a static WebP background and a few key assets animated with CSS, and made information-focused sections fully static. The site became much lighter, kept its intended look, and no longer needed a loading screen.

SHIPPING RESULT

Vibe coding cut updates from a month to 5 days, and performance rose 55→92

The optimization raised the Lighthouse score from 55 to 92, cut page weight by 94%, and lowered loading drop-off from 24% to 6%. The vibe-coding workflow also reduced development and QA time for updates from about a month to five days. I documented the process from a designer's perspective and shared it company-wide, where it became an internal best practice.

LEARNING

Ownership drove results; next time, I'll plan around the team's capacity

As both product manager and product designer for the first time, I led the project from planning to launch, ahead of schedule. It earned me the company's highest performance rating, but I was proudest of getting the team to launch despite losing frontend support.

Looking back, I found two gaps in how I planned. First, I treated agreement as capacity. Teammates said yes to a fast timeline, but they were also managing several other projects. Second, I planned for the expected, not the variable. Urgent requests often came up at our company, and without a buffer, the team felt rushed midway. Going forward, I'll check each person's workload before setting timelines and build in buffer for unexpected requests, so speed stays sustainable.
